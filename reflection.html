Reflection on my teaching development

This section discusses the main connections between my course activities and identifies areas I would like to develop further in my teaching.

A WORKING SYNTHESIS

Explaining the concept.
Checking understanding.
Improving the approach.

Reflection based on coursework completed through October 29, 2026.

Why learning about college teaching matters

Knowledge of a subject is necessary for teaching, but it does not explain how every student will understand that subject. For example, a programming solution that seems straightforward to an experienced learner may contain several unfamiliar steps for a beginner. Learning about college teaching helps me consider those steps, choose suitable activities, and examine students’ responses. It also provides a basis for revising an explanation when it does not address the difficulty students are experiencing.

Student interest and evidence of learning

My teaching philosophy discusses the importance of developing interest through practical examples and encouraging students to ask questions. The student-learning presentation adds another consideration: a student may perform well on a familiar task but have difficulty using the concept in a new situation. These activities help me consider why an interesting explanation should be followed by practice and a check for understanding. I would therefore examine what students can explain and apply after the topic has been discussed.

Providing guidance according to students’ needs

The Engaged Teaching Inventory records Facilitator as my highest score, with Expert and Personal Model also scoring highly. My PCK response discusses clear explanations and dividing complex ideas into smaller parts. Together, these activities help me consider how much guidance a student may need. I would provide an example when students are unfamiliar with a task and gradually give them more responsibility as they become able to explain the process themselves.

Considering the amount of content in a class

My Java class plan includes explanations, demonstrations, discussion, and an ungraded quiz. However, it also covers several topics in 50 minutes. For students who are new to programming, this amount of content may be difficult to process before moving to the next topic. This connects the planning activity to cognitive load. A revised plan could focus on fewer concepts and provide more time for students to practice and explain their answers.

Connecting learning outcomes to assessment

The course-planning exercise identifies goals and outcomes, while the syllabus review examines whether students have enough information about assessment. The proposed CS syllabus combines these decisions in a more complete course design. These activities show why outcomes need to be connected to specific tasks and clear assessment criteria. I would develop this further by identifying where students can practice each outcome and receive feedback before completing a graded assessment.
